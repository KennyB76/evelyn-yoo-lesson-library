§ 1. Why Kenny's Way? "Add & Subtract Inside" vs "Add to Both Sides"

When you complete the square on $ax^2 + bx + c = 0$ where $a \neq 1$, two methods exist. They both work mathematically, but one is much safer for students.

⚠ The Common Mistake (textbook way): Students factor out $a$, then "add the magic number to both sides" — but they forget that $a$ is still multiplying the bracket. So they add $\dfrac{b^2}{4a^2}$ to the right side instead of $a \cdot \dfrac{b^2}{4a^2} = \dfrac{b^2}{4a}$. The equation is now broken, and Evelyn won't know why her answer is wrong.

Example of the danger:

$3(x^2 + 2x) = 5$. Inside, the magic number is $1$.
❌ WRONG (both-sides method): $3(x^2 + 2x + 1) = 5 + 1$. (Forgot to multiply $1$ by $3$. Right side should be $5 + 3$.)
✔ RIGHT: $3(x^2 + 2x + 1) = 5 + 3$.

✔ Kenny's Way (Add & Subtract Inside): Don't touch the right side. Inside the bracket, add the magic number AND subtract it — the bracket is mathematically unchanged (you added zero). Then peel out the − piece, multiplying by $a$ as you take it out.

Why this is safer: The leading coefficient $a$ stays attached to whatever leaves the bracket, so you can't forget to multiply it.
§ 2. Completing the Square — Full Proof of the Quadratic Formula

Start with the general form. Use Kenny's "add & subtract inside" technique step by step. By the end you will have derived the quadratic formula yourself.

Start $ax^2 + bx + c = 0,\quad a \neq 0$
Step 1 Factor $a$ from the first two terms only:
$\quad a\!\left(x^2 + \dfrac{b}{a}x\right) + c = 0$
Step 2 Magic number $= \left(\dfrac{1}{2}\cdot\dfrac{b}{a}\right)^2 = \dfrac{b^2}{4a^2}$. Add and subtract it INSIDE the bracket (net change = $0$):
$\quad a\!\left(x^2 + \dfrac{b}{a}x + \dfrac{b^2}{4a^2} - \dfrac{b^2}{4a^2}\right) + c = 0$
Step 3 Peel the − piece OUT of the bracket. Multiply it by $a$ as it leaves:
$\quad a\!\left(x^2 + \dfrac{b}{a}x + \dfrac{b^2}{4a^2}\right) - a\cdot\dfrac{b^2}{4a^2} + c = 0$
$\quad a\!\left(x^2 + \dfrac{b}{a}x + \dfrac{b^2}{4a^2}\right) - \dfrac{b^2}{4a} + c = 0$
Step 4 The bracket is now a perfect square: $\left(x + \dfrac{b}{2a}\right)^2$.
$\quad a\!\left(x + \dfrac{b}{2a}\right)^2 = \dfrac{b^2}{4a} - c$
Step 5 Common denominator on the right: $\dfrac{b^2}{4a} - c = \dfrac{b^2 - 4ac}{4a}$. Then divide both sides by $a$:
$\quad \left(x + \dfrac{b}{2a}\right)^2 = \dfrac{b^2 - 4ac}{4a^2}$
Step 6 Square root both sides (don't forget $\pm$):
$\quad x + \dfrac{b}{2a} = \pm\dfrac{\sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}$
Step 7 Subtract $\dfrac{b}{2a}$ from both sides:
$\quad x = \dfrac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}$
Quadratic Formula:    $x = \dfrac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}$

Big idea: The quadratic formula is nothing but "completing the square done once for the general case." If you can do completing the square, you already know where the formula came from.

§ 3. Three Methods — Which One When?

Always start by computing the discriminant $D = b^2 - 4ac$. The shape of $D$ (and the parity of $b$) tells you which method is cleanest.

Decision Tree:
  1. Compute $D = b^2 - 4ac$.
  2. If $D$ is a perfect square ($0, 1, 4, 9, 16, 25, 36, 49, 64, 81, 100, \ldots$)  →  FACTOR.  (Easiest, no radicals.)
  3. Else if $b$ is even  →  COMPLETE THE SQUARE.  (Half of $b$ is a clean integer — no fractions.)
  4. Else  →  QUADRATIC FORMULA.  (Always works.)
⚠ Note: If $b$ is even, the quadratic formula will reduce at the end (the $2$'s cancel). So completing the square gets you there with cleaner arithmetic. Save the formula for when $b$ is odd or $a \neq 1$ in a messy way.

Method 1 — Factor

Use when $D = b^2 - 4ac$ is a perfect square. Roots are rational.

Find two numbers whose product is $ac$ and whose sum is $b$. Split the middle term, factor by grouping (or use the standard "two binomials" form when $a = 1$).

Method 2 — Complete the Square (Kenny's "Inside" Style)

Use when $D$ is NOT a perfect square AND $b$ is even.

If $a = 1$: just take half of $b$, square it, add & subtract inside.
If $a \neq 1$: factor $a$ first, then add & subtract inside the bracket as in § 2.

Method 3 — Quadratic Formula

Use when $b$ is odd, or when $a \neq 1$ and the bracket gets messy.

$x = \dfrac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}$. Always works, but most arithmetic. Simplify the radical at the end.

§ 4. Worked Examples — One Per Method
Example 1 — FACTOR ($D$ is a perfect square)

Solve: $x^2 - 2x - 15 = 0$

Step 1 (decision): $D = (-2)^2 - 4(1)(-15) = 4 + 60 = 64 = 8^2$.  ⇒  Perfect square → FACTOR.

Step 2: Two numbers with product $-15$ and sum $-2$: $-5$ and $+3$.

Step 3: $(x - 5)(x + 3) = 0$.

Answer: $\mathbf{x = 5}$ or $\mathbf{x = -3}$.

Example 2 — COMPLETE THE SQUARE ($b$ even, $D$ not perfect square)

Solve: $x^2 + 6x + 4 = 0$

Step 1 (decision): $D = 36 - 16 = 20$ (not a perfect square). $b = 6$ is even.  ⇒  COMPLETE THE SQUARE.

Step 2 (Kenny's inside method): Magic number $= \left(\dfrac{6}{2}\right)^2 = 9$. Add & subtract inside: $x^2 + 6x + 9 - 9 + 4 = 0$.

Step 3: Group: $(x + 3)^2 - 5 = 0$  ⇒  $(x + 3)^2 = 5$.

Step 4: $x + 3 = \pm\sqrt{5}$  ⇒  $\mathbf{x = -3 \pm \sqrt{5}}$.

Cross-check with formula: $x = \dfrac{-6 \pm \sqrt{20}}{2} = \dfrac{-6 \pm 2\sqrt{5}}{2} = -3 \pm \sqrt{5}$. ✔ Same answer, but completing the square skipped the reducing step.

Example 3 — COMPLETE THE SQUARE with $a \neq 1$ (Kenny's "Inside" Style)

Solve: $3x^2 - 2x - 3 = 0$

Step 1 (decision): $D = 4 + 36 = 40$ (not perfect square). $b = -2$ is even but $a = 3 \neq 1$. Try Kenny's inside method (or quadratic formula).

Step 2: Factor out $3$ from the first two terms: $3\!\left(x^2 - \dfrac{2}{3}x\right) - 3 = 0$.

Step 3: Magic number $= \left(\dfrac{1}{2}\cdot\dfrac{2}{3}\right)^2 = \dfrac{1}{9}$. Add & subtract inside: $3\!\left(x^2 - \dfrac{2}{3}x + \dfrac{1}{9} - \dfrac{1}{9}\right) - 3 = 0$.

Step 4: Peel the $-\dfrac{1}{9}$ out (multiply by $3$): $3\!\left(x - \dfrac{1}{3}\right)^2 - \dfrac{1}{3} - 3 = 0$.

Step 5: $3\!\left(x - \dfrac{1}{3}\right)^2 = \dfrac{10}{3}$  ⇒  $\left(x - \dfrac{1}{3}\right)^2 = \dfrac{10}{9}$.

Step 6: $x - \dfrac{1}{3} = \pm\dfrac{\sqrt{10}}{3}$  ⇒  $\mathbf{x = \dfrac{1 \pm \sqrt{10}}{3}}$.

Example 4 — QUADRATIC FORMULA ($b$ odd)

Solve: $2x^2 + 3x - 1 = 0$

Step 1 (decision): $D = 9 + 8 = 17$ (not perfect square). $b = 3$ is odd.  ⇒  QUADRATIC FORMULA.

Step 2: $x = \dfrac{-3 \pm \sqrt{17}}{2(2)} = \dfrac{-3 \pm \sqrt{17}}{4}$.

Answer: $\mathbf{x = \dfrac{-3 \pm \sqrt{17}}{4}}$.

Example 5 — The "Reduce at the End" Trap (when $b$ is even but you used the formula)

Solve: $x^2 + 4x - 1 = 0$ — using the formula, then verify by completing the square.

Formula: $x = \dfrac{-4 \pm \sqrt{16 + 4}}{2} = \dfrac{-4 \pm \sqrt{20}}{2} = \dfrac{-4 \pm 2\sqrt{5}}{2} = -2 \pm \sqrt{5}$. (Two reducing steps.)

Completing the square (cleaner): Magic $= 4$. $x^2 + 4x + 4 - 4 - 1 = 0$  ⇒  $(x + 2)^2 = 5$  ⇒  $x = -2 \pm \sqrt{5}$. ✔

Lesson: When $b$ is even, completing the square gives you the simplified answer in one shot. The formula gives the same answer but you must reduce.
